Start here: diagnose before you repair

Good repair starts with evidence, not guesswork. A symptom can have several causes: a slow computer might have a full drive, a failing drive, a background update, overheating, or simply too many open applications. Replacing parts before checking the cause wastes money and can create new problems.

  1. Identify the exact device. Record manufacturer, full model number, model year if known, operating system version, and hardware revision. Similar model names can have different internals.
  2. Describe the fault precisely. Record what happens, what you expected, when it began, whether it happens every time, and any exact error text.
  3. Look for recent changes. Updates, new apps, drops, liquid exposure, new cables, and changed settings can provide useful clues.
  4. Back up important data. Verify that the backup actually contains files you can open. Do this before resets, firmware updates, reinstallations, or invasive repair.
  5. Check warranty and support. Opening a device can affect warranty coverage or introduce risk. Read the manufacturer's terms before proceeding.
  6. Change one thing at a time. Record the result and reverse the change if it makes things worse.

If a device is wet, unusually hot, physically damaged, making electrical noises, or has a damaged battery, stop using it and seek qualified help. Do not keep powering it on to “see if it works.”

1. Build a useful fault report

A clear report makes free community or Help Desk support much more effective. Include the device model, operating system, symptoms, exact error messages, when it started, what changed recently, and what you have already tried. Include photos only after checking that they do not show private information, serial numbers, addresses, account names, or recovery codes.

Example report structure

  • Device: Manufacturer and exact model.
  • System: Operating system and version.
  • Problem: One or two sentences describing the symptom.
  • Frequency: Always, sometimes, or only under a particular condition.
  • Recent changes: Updates, new accessories, drops, or none known.
  • Checks performed: Safe checks and their results.
  • Important: Whether data is backed up and whether there are signs of heat, liquid, or battery damage.

2. Safe first checks

Power and charging

  • Check that the wall outlet and external power adapter are undamaged. If safe and permitted, test the outlet with a known-working low-power device.
  • Check the cable and connector for visible damage, bent pins, fraying, discoloration, or unusual heat. Stop using damaged accessories.
  • Use a charger that meets the manufacturer's specifications. A connector fitting physically does not prove the charger is suitable.
  • Do not force connectors, insert metal objects into ports, or open a power adapter.

Software and startup

  • Restart once if the device is responsive and there are no signs of physical or electrical damage.
  • Install updates from the operating system's official updater or manufacturer.
  • Check storage space and the built-in task or activity monitor.
  • Review recently installed apps and browser extensions. Remove only software you recognize and no longer need.
  • Run the built-in security scan if you see unexpected pop-ups or unknown processes.

3. Common symptoms and what they can mean

Device is slow

Check CPU, memory, disk activity, available storage, and temperature. Test after a restart and with unnecessary apps closed. If it slows down only during demanding tasks, the hardware may be reaching its performance limit. Avoid miracle “speed booster” utilities and scripts that disable security services.

Random restarts or shutdowns

Record the time and workload, check system reliability or event logs, install official updates, and look for overheating or power-adapter problems. Repeated sudden shutdowns, burning smells, electrical crackling, or unusual heat require stopping use and professional assessment.

Wi-Fi disconnects

Check whether other devices on the same network are affected. Restart the router only if you are authorized to do so. Forgetting and rejoining a network may help, but make sure you know the password first. Do not change managed workplace, school, hospital, or public-network settings without permission.

App crashes

Update the app, restart the device, check available storage, and look for a service-status notice from the app maker. If one app alone fails, its support page is more relevant than reinstalling the whole operating system.

Screen or display problems

Check brightness, display selection, external monitor cables, and whether the issue appears before the operating system loads. Cracked screens, exposed wiring, liquid damage, and flickering accompanied by heat should be assessed by a repair professional.

4. Cleaning and routine maintenance

Keep devices on a clean, dry, stable surface. Keep ventilation openings unobstructed. Clean external surfaces with a soft, lint-free cloth and follow the manufacturer's cleaning guidance. Power down and unplug equipment before external cleaning where the manual instructs. Never spray liquid directly into a device, use household cleaners on screens unless approved, or push tools into vents and ports.

For laptop fans, internal dust removal, thermal paste, screen replacement, charging-port replacement, and other internal work, use the exact service manual and assess whether the job requires specialist tools. Do not open power supplies or mains-powered equipment. Lithium-ion batteries can be hazardous if punctured, crushed, bent, overheated, or mishandled.

5. Parts, compatibility, and repair economics

Before buying a part, verify the exact model and part number. Check dimensions, connector type, voltage/current requirements where applicable, firmware compatibility, and whether the part is user-replaceable. A product listing that says “fits many models” is not sufficient evidence.

  • Compare the total cost: part, shipping, tools, consumables, and possible professional labor.
  • Check whether the replacement part is new, refurbished, used, or aftermarket.
  • Ask whether the repair restores full function and whether calibration or software pairing is required.
  • Compare repair cost with a safe, supported replacement—not simply the cheapest listing.
  • Keep the old part until the repair is tested and the return period has passed.

Repair is not always cheaper, but a diagnosis can prevent replacing a device unnecessarily. Reuse, warranty service, community repair events, and manufacturer repair programs may reduce costs.

6. When to stop and get qualified help

  • Swollen, punctured, leaking, hot, or damaged rechargeable battery.
  • Liquid inside a powered device, corrosion, burning smell, smoke, sparks, or exposed mains wiring.
  • Power-supply, wall-socket, household wiring, or high-voltage equipment faults.
  • Microwave, CRT display, or other equipment that can retain dangerous electrical energy even when unplugged.
  • Any repair requiring soldering, specialist calibration, or safety testing beyond your training.
  • Important data that is not backed up, especially when a drive may be failing.

Do not attempt to open or discharge hazardous components. Ask a qualified repair technician or the manufacturer.

7. Free repair references

  • iFixit repair guides — model-specific community guides.
  • Dell Support — manuals, diagnostics, drivers, and warranty information.
  • Lenovo Support — model-specific documentation.
  • Microsoft Support — Windows troubleshooting and recovery.
  • Apple Support — official device guidance.
  • Google Help — Android and Chromebook support.

Use the manufacturer's manual for the exact device before following any repair procedure. Free documentation does not mean every repair is safe for an untrained person.
