Start here: privacy foundations

These guides focus on personal safety, informed choices, and defensive privacy. No single app, VPN, browser, or setting makes someone completely anonymous or untraceable. Use privacy tools responsibly and follow the rules of devices and networks you use. Start by deciding what information you want to protect and from whom.

1. DNS and encrypted DNS

DNS translates a domain name into the network address a device needs to connect. Traditional DNS is often unencrypted between your device and resolver. DNS over HTTPS (DoH) and DNS over TLS (DoT) encrypt that connection to the resolver, but the resolver itself can still process the queries.

Safe setup checklist

  1. Choose a resolver whose privacy policy and operator you understand.
  2. Use your browser or operating system's built-in encrypted-DNS settings where available.
  3. Avoid installing random DNS certificates or configuration profiles.
  4. Check that your browser is actually using the intended resolver after setup.
  5. Remember that managed networks may enforce their own DNS settings.

Cloudflare: configure DoH in common browsers · Cloudflare: Linux setup guide · Cloudflare resolver check

Encrypted DNS protects DNS traffic on the path to the resolver. It does not hide every website connection or make browsing anonymous.

2. VPNs: setup and limitations

A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a VPN provider. Websites generally see the VPN server's public IP instead of your network's public IP. Your VPN provider becomes an important party you must trust.

Before connecting

  • Download the official app from the provider's official website or your device's trusted app store.
  • Keep the app updated and use its default secure protocol unless you have a clear reason to change it.
  • Learn what its kill switch and DNS protection settings do; test them using the provider's documentation.
  • Check whether you are using a full-device VPN app or only a browser extension. They protect different traffic.
  • Do not assume a VPN hides your identity when you sign in to an account or share identifying details.

3. Tor Browser

Tor Browser routes browsing through the Tor network and includes privacy protections intended to reduce tracking and browser fingerprinting. It is not a magic invisibility cloak, and it does not protect every app on a device.

Safer use principles

  • Get Tor Browser only from the official Tor Project website and keep it updated.
  • Keep its built-in privacy settings and avoid adding extensions that make your browser more unique.
  • Use HTTPS websites and be cautious about opening downloaded documents in external applications.
  • Signing in to a personal account can identify you to that service even when using Tor.
  • Understand that websites may block Tor connections and that connection speed can vary.

4. Personal OPSEC (operational security)

OPSEC is the habit of thinking about what information could expose something you want to keep private. For everyday personal safety, use it to reduce accidental oversharing and protect accounts—not to promise that someone can become untraceable.

A simple threat-model worksheet

  1. Asset: What are you protecting? Examples include account access, private photos, location, or personal conversations.
  2. Threat: What could realistically go wrong? Examples include phishing, account takeover, doxxing, or a lost device.
  3. Exposure: Where could the information leak? Consider public profiles, app permissions, metadata, reused passwords, and shared devices.
  4. Controls: Use unique passwords, a password manager, multi-factor authentication, updates, backups, and careful sharing.
  5. Review: Revisit your choices when your device, accounts, or circumstances change.

Do not post private addresses, school or workplace details, real-time location, identity documents, recovery codes, or screenshots containing account tokens.

5. Browser privacy

  • Keep your browser and extensions updated.
  • Remove extensions you do not recognize or need.
  • Review site permissions for camera, microphone, location, and notifications.
  • Use built-in tracking protection and be cautious with third-party cookies.
  • Understand that browser fingerprinting can combine many ordinary settings into a recognizable pattern.

Mozilla: privacy and security help

6. Password managers and multi-factor authentication

  • Use a different, strong password for every important account.
  • Store passwords in a reputable password manager and protect it with a strong master passphrase.
  • Enable multi-factor authentication or passkeys where supported.
  • Keep recovery codes somewhere private and secure; never share them with anyone.
  • Be alert for fake sign-in pages and unexpected password-reset messages.

7. Device encryption and backups

Device encryption helps protect stored files if a device is lost or accessed without authorization. Check your operating system's official documentation to understand whether encryption is enabled and where recovery keys are stored.

  • Keep recovery keys separate from the device they unlock.
  • Maintain backups of important files and test that you can restore them.
  • Use encrypted backups for sensitive material and protect backup credentials.
  • Keep the operating system and security software updated.

8. Metadata and safer sharing

Photos and documents can contain extra information such as timestamps, device details, author names, or location metadata. Screenshots can also reveal usernames, notifications, browser tabs, or account identifiers.

  • Review a file before posting it publicly.
  • Use built-in privacy or export settings to remove metadata when appropriate.
  • Crop or redact account details from screenshots before sharing.
  • Do not upload identity documents, private messages, or images that reveal your location.

9. Linux and network basics

Learn the purpose of DNS, IP addresses, routing, firewalls, TLS certificates, and software repositories before changing system settings. Prefer official distribution documentation and signed software repositories. Make a backup before changing network configuration.

  • Install security updates through your distribution's supported update mechanism.
  • Use trusted repositories and verify software sources.
  • Keep firewall rules understandable and document changes.
  • Do not paste commands from unknown sources into a root terminal.

10. Windows 10 and 11 performance

Start by identifying the bottleneck instead of installing a random “PC optimizer.” Open Task Manager with Ctrl + Shift + Esc and inspect CPU, Memory, Disk, and Startup apps. Check which process is actually using resources.

  1. Install Windows and supported driver updates through Windows Update or the device manufacturer's official support page.
  2. Open Settings → System → Storage and review temporary files before deleting anything. Check the files you are removing.
  3. Open Task Manager → Startup apps and disable only apps you recognize and do not need at startup.
  4. Uninstall applications you no longer use, and close apps or browser tabs you are not using.
  5. For a plugged-in gaming or rendering session, you can test a higher-performance power mode. It uses more energy and may increase heat and fan noise.
  6. Run a Windows Security scan if unexpected background activity or pop-ups appear.

Windows 10 note: Microsoft's standard support ended October 14, 2025. A Windows 10 device without an applicable extended security update program no longer receives normal free security fixes. Check Microsoft's current support guidance before relying on it online.

11. Linux laptop optimization (including low-RAM systems)

Linux distributions differ, so menu names and tools vary. On a slower laptop, measure first: use the system monitor to inspect CPU, memory, swap, disk activity, and the processes using the most resources.

  • Keep the distribution and applications updated using its normal, signed package repositories.
  • Review startup applications and background services. Disable only services you understand and do not rely on.
  • Use a browser tab-suspension feature or close tabs you no longer need; browsers can use substantial memory.
  • If the desktop environment is too heavy for the hardware, compare a supported lightweight desktop environment before reinstalling anything.
  • Check whether the storage drive is nearly full or reporting errors. Keep free space available for updates and temporary files.
  • Linux uses spare RAM for caches. High “used” memory is not automatically a problem; check available memory and swap activity before changing anything.
  • For an older laptop, a compatible SSD or RAM upgrade can help more than aggressive software tweaks. Check the exact model's service manual and memory limits first.

Avoid copying mystery “debloat” scripts, disabling security services, or applying random sysctl tweaks. Change one setting at a time and keep a way to undo it.

Linux kernel: administrator guide · Linux kernel: memory management

12. macOS performance and battery

  • Use Activity Monitor to identify CPU, memory, energy, and disk-heavy apps.
  • Review System Settings → General → Login Items and remove only items you recognize and do not need.
  • Check System Settings → General → Storage and review large files before removing them.
  • Install macOS updates and app updates from trusted sources.
  • If battery life is poor, check Battery settings and Battery Health where available. Heat and constant high workloads can affect battery longevity.
  • Keep a current backup before major updates or troubleshooting.

Apple: Mac support

13. Android phones and tablets

  • Install Android and app updates through the device's normal settings and official app store.
  • Open Settings → Battery → Battery usage to identify apps using unusually high power.
  • Review storage and remove downloads or apps you no longer need. Back up important photos and files before deleting them.
  • Restrict background activity only for apps where delayed notifications or syncing will not cause problems.
  • Restart the device if an app or service becomes stuck, and update or reinstall that app if the problem repeats.
  • Avoid “RAM booster,” “phone cleaner,” and task-killer apps that promise impossible speed gains. Android manages memory itself.
  • Do not root a phone or install unofficial firmware just to make it faster; that can weaken security and break updates.

Google: Android Help · Google Pixel: battery troubleshooting

14. iPhone and iPad optimization

  • Check Settings → General → iPhone/iPad Storage and review Apple's storage recommendations.
  • Offload unused apps when you want to free app storage while retaining their documents and data.
  • Check Settings → Battery for apps using more energy than expected.
  • Install supported iOS or iPadOS updates and keep enough free storage for the update process.
  • Use Low Power Mode when battery life matters more than background activity or some visual features.
  • Back up before major troubleshooting. Avoid unofficial “cleaner” apps and jailbreak tweaks advertised as performance fixes.

Apple: If your iPhone or iPad is running slowly

15. Chromebook and ChromeOS

  • Install ChromeOS updates from Settings → About ChromeOS.
  • Remove extensions you do not recognize or no longer need. Extensions can consume memory and access browsing data.
  • Close unused tabs and apps. Use the Chromebook diagnostics and task manager tools to identify resource-heavy processes.
  • Check available storage and remove unneeded downloads after backing up anything important.
  • Powerwash is a reset, not a routine speed tweak. Back up local files and understand that local data is removed before using it.

16. Gaming performance: PC and laptop

Measure performance before changing settings. Average FPS is useful, but frame-time spikes and stutter can matter just as much.

  1. Update the game and use graphics drivers from Windows Update or the official GPU manufacturer's site.
  2. Lower demanding settings one at a time: resolution, ray tracing, shadows, reflections, and texture quality. Texture settings depend heavily on available video memory.
  3. Use an FPS cap that your device can sustain smoothly instead of forcing the GPU to run at maximum all the time.
  4. Close unneeded launchers, browser tabs, recording tools, and overlays while testing.
  5. Keep laptop vents unobstructed and use the device on a firm surface. If temperatures are high or performance drops after a few minutes, stop and investigate cooling rather than pushing the hardware harder.
  6. Install games on a healthy SSD when possible. An SSD can improve loading and reduce asset-streaming pauses, but it does not replace a faster GPU.
  7. Compare results using the same game scene and settings. Change one setting at a time so you know what helped.

Avoid overclocking, unofficial driver packs, registry “FPS boosts,” and scripts that disable security or operating-system services. These can make systems unstable or less secure.

17. RAM, swap, and memory optimization

RAM is working space for apps. When memory is tight, operating systems reclaim caches, compress memory, or use swap. That is normal behavior—not proof that the system needs a third-party cleaner.

  • Find the apps and browser tabs using the most memory before closing anything.
  • Keep some storage free because operating systems need room for updates, temporary files, and swap.
  • Do not repeatedly clear caches or force-close system processes. The system may simply rebuild what you removed.
  • On Linux, compare available memory and swap activity rather than treating all cached memory as wasted.
  • Before buying RAM, check the exact model, supported capacity, memory type, number of slots, and whether memory is soldered.
  • For gaming, check whether the limit is system RAM, video memory, CPU, GPU, or storage. More RAM does not fix every performance problem.

18. Device maintenance and safe troubleshooting

Use a diagnosis-first approach. Record the exact model and symptom, note when the problem started, and check warranty or manufacturer support before opening a device.

  1. Describe the fault: What happens, what does not happen, and whether there is an error message.
  2. Check simple causes: Confirm the correct charger or cable, inspect external ports without inserting metal objects, and try a known-good accessory if available.
  3. Back up data: Copy important files before resets, reinstallations, firmware updates, or repair work.
  4. Use built-in diagnostics: Check the manufacturer's diagnostic app, system logs, and official troubleshooting pages.
  5. Check the exact manual: Match the full model number and hardware revision. Similar-looking devices can use different parts and procedures.
  6. Document changes: Change one thing at a time and record the result so you can reverse it.

Safety boundary: Internal electronics repair is not suitable for every user. Do not open power supplies, mains-powered equipment, or battery packs. Do not press, puncture, bend, or attempt to repair a swollen or damaged rechargeable battery. If a device is unusually hot, damaged, leaking, or smoking, stop using it and get help from a responsible adult or qualified repair professional. Follow the manufacturer's safety instructions.
