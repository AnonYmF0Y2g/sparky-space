1. Start with a threat model

Security is about reducing realistic risks, not becoming invisible. Identify what matters, who might target it, and what protections are proportionate. For most people, common risks include phishing, reused passwords, account recovery abuse, lost devices, malicious downloads, and accidental oversharing.

  1. Protect: email, banking, social accounts, private files, photos, and device access.
  2. Consider likely threats: scams, account takeover, malware, theft, and privacy leaks.
  3. Find weak points: reused passwords, outdated software, public posts, excessive app permissions, and unsecured backups.
  4. Choose controls: unique passwords, MFA/passkeys, updates, backups, and cautious sharing.
  5. Review: repeat after a device change, account incident, or major life change.

2. Secure your email first

Email often controls password resets for other accounts, so it deserves strong protection. Use a unique password or passkey, enable multi-factor authentication, review recovery email addresses and phone numbers, and check active sessions. Save recovery codes in a private place separate from the device. Do not share codes with anyone, including someone claiming to be support.

If you suspect compromise, use the provider's official account-recovery page from a trusted device. Change reused passwords on other services, revoke unfamiliar sessions and app access, and check forwarding rules or filters you did not create.

3. Passwords, passkeys, and MFA

  • Use a different password for every account. A password manager can generate and store long random passwords.
  • Protect the password manager with a strong master passphrase and MFA where available.
  • Prefer passkeys or authenticator-based MFA over SMS when the service supports them and you can maintain account recovery.
  • Keep recovery methods current and protect backup codes.
  • Never approve a login prompt you did not initiate.

MFA reduces risk but does not make phishing impossible. Check the website address before entering credentials, and avoid signing in from links in unexpected messages.

4. Keep devices and software trustworthy

Enable automatic security updates where practical. Download apps from official stores or the developer's verified site. Remove software you no longer use, review browser extensions, and avoid pirated software or “cracks.” Do not run unknown scripts as administrator or root. Back up important data and test restoring a file.

When a device no longer receives security updates, reduce sensitive use and plan a supported replacement or alternative. A device can still turn on while being unsafe for sensitive online activity.

5. Home and public Wi-Fi

Use WPA2-AES or WPA3 on a home router where supported, set a unique administrator password, update router firmware through the maker's official process, and disable remote administration if you do not need it. Use a guest network for visitors and untrusted smart devices where available. Do not share router credentials publicly.

On public Wi-Fi, use HTTPS websites, keep sharing features off, and avoid installing certificates or configuration profiles supplied by unknown people. A VPN can protect traffic between your device and its provider, but the provider becomes a party you must trust. A VPN does not make you anonymous or protect you from phishing.

6. DNS, VPNs, Tor, and their limits

DNS translates website names into network addresses. Encrypted DNS protects the connection to the chosen resolver, but the resolver can still process queries. A VPN tunnels selected traffic through a provider; it changes who can observe parts of the connection, not whether you can be identified when logged in. Tor Browser routes browser traffic through the Tor network and reduces some tracking, but does not protect every app or make unsafe downloads safe.

  • Use official downloads and documentation.
  • Do not install random root certificates or configuration profiles.
  • Do not combine many privacy tools without understanding conflicts and leaks.
  • Follow workplace, school, care, and public-network rules.
  • Do not promise yourself or others perfect anonymity.

Official references: Mozilla privacy help, Tor Project Support, Proton VPN support.

7. Privacy when sharing photos and documents

Files can contain timestamps, device details, author names, or location metadata. Screenshots can reveal notifications, usernames, browser tabs, account identifiers, or private messages. Review a file before publishing, use built-in privacy/export settings where appropriate, and redact sensitive details. Never post recovery codes, identity documents, private addresses, or real-time location.

8. What to do after a suspected compromise

  1. Use a trusted device and reach the service through its official website or app.
  2. Secure the email account used for recovery.
  3. Change the affected password to a unique one and revoke unknown sessions.
  4. Review recovery details, connected apps, forwarding rules, and recent security activity.
  5. Enable MFA or passkeys and save recovery options safely.
  6. Run a reputable security scan and update the device.
  7. Warn contacts if scam messages were sent from your account.

If money, identity documents, or threats are involved, contact the relevant bank, service provider, or appropriate local authority promptly.

Further help

For a website or device issue, include the exact error, device type, and what happened. Do not include passwords, one-time codes, recovery keys, or private account tokens.
